## 🏛️ Project Overview

**Grammar-Pro** is an editorial learning platform designed for secondary and high school English learners preparing for high-stakes examinations (Thanaweya Amma / Cambridge / IGCSE / British & American Diplomas).

It provides:
1. **The Master Rules Ledger:** Complete coverage of all 13 core syllabus rules with active/passive formations, keywords, time clauses, and cause-effect conjunctions.
2. **The 3 Golden Prohibitions on Continuous Tense:** In-depth rules on Momentary Verbs, Counted Items, and Stative Verbs.
3. **Dual-Meaning Verbs Lab:** 10 predicates with dual stative vs action meanings (`think, have, see, taste, smell, feel, look, weigh, measure, be`).
4. **Interactive Verb Workbench:** Real-time grammatical analysis of any English verb.
5. **Location & Return Board:** Visual interactive journey simulator for `has been to`, `has gone to`, and `has been in`.
6. **Since vs For Speed Sorter:** Rapid interactive classification for 12 core time expressions.
7. **The 17-Question Examination Studio:** Real interactive test interface with instant validation, audio feedback, and the **official explanatory notes integrated verbatim**.
8. **Official Notes Dossier:** A dedicated study sheet presenting all 17 answers and their authoritative notes side-by-side.

## 📝 17 Examination Answers & Official Notes Key

| Item | Question Key | Correct Option | Master Rationale |
| :---: | :--- | :---: | :--- |
| **01** | *They ___ each other for a long time.* | **(b) have known** | *"Know" is a stative verb describing a mental state, so it cannot be used in continuous forms (have been knowing is incorrect); the duration marker "for a long time" connecting the past to the present requires the present perfect simple.* |
| **02** | *A: Have you arrived home ___? B: No...* | **(a) yet** | *Used at the end of questions and negative statements in the present perfect tense to ask or indicate whether an expected action has taken place up to the moment of speaking.* |
| **03** | *Omar can’t take part... ___ he has broken his leg.* | **(a) since** | *Acts as a subordinating conjunction of cause meaning "because" or "as", introducing the explanation for why Omar cannot participate in the competition.* |
| **04** | *As soon as he ___ home, he’s going to take a shower.* | **(c) has arrived** | *In time clauses referring to future events (introduced by connectors like as soon as, when, or after), English grammar requires a present tense (present simple or present perfect) rather than a past or future tense.* |
| **05** | *He ___ to the beach. His skin looks dark.* | **(b) has been** | *"Has been to" indicates that a person visited a place and has already returned; his dark skin is the present evidence of a completed visit, whereas "has gone" would mean he is still at the beach.* |
| **06** | *Which sentence means Lean is no longer in New York?* | **(b) Lean has been to New York** | *"Has been to" denotes a completed journey from which the subject has returned, confirming she is no longer there.* |
| **07** | *Complete: “___ such an intelligent student”.* | **(c) Hala has never seen** | *Follows the standard present perfect experiential construction (subject + has/have never seen + such a/an + adjective + noun); option (b) contains an incorrect double negative (haven't never).* |
| **08** | *I ___ all Adel Emam films.* | **(b) have seen** | *When stating the total number or quantity of items completed (here, all Adel Emam films), the present perfect simple must be used instead of the present perfect continuous.* |
| **09** | *She ___ the guitar since 2012. She retired a month ago.* | **(d) had been playing** | *The past time marker "She retired a month ago" establishes a completed past event; an action that took place continuously over a period up to that specific point in the past requires the past perfect continuous.* |
| **10** | *She ___ the guitar since 2012.* | **(c) has been playing** | *Unlike question 9, there is no statement that she stopped playing, indicating the activity started in 2012 and is still continuing into the present moment.* |
| **11** | *I ___ my boss several times last month.* | **(d) emailed** | *"Last month" specifies a completed, closed time period in the past, which mandates the past simple tense.* |
| **12** | *I ___ to London twice before.* | **(a) have been** | *Counting the frequency of life experiences ("twice before") up to the current moment requires the present perfect simple.* |
| **13** | *Which sentence is grammatically correct?* | **(d) Mum has been doing the housework since we left for school** | *"Contain" and "taste" (describing quality) are stative verbs and cannot take the continuous form (ruling out a and b). A past time clause like "When I was a child" cannot pair with the present perfect (ruling out c). (d) correctly links an ongoing activity to a past starting point using since + past simple.* |
| **14** | *Complete: “Since 2014, ___”* | **(c) a & b** | *Verbs representing long-term activities like work and live can be used in either the present perfect simple (have worked) or the present perfect continuous (have been working) with "since" to convey essentially the same meaning.* |
| **15** | *You look scared. ___ anything frightening?* | **(c) Have you seen** | *The visible present effect or emotion ("You look scared") calls for the present perfect simple to ask about the recent past experience causing it.* |
| **16** | *Sama ___ met such a nice person.* | **(d) has never** | *The subject Sama is third-person singular (requiring has), and the sentence expresses a lifetime experience using never with such a/an.* |
| **17** | *Ayman has drunk five coffees ___.* | **(b) so far** | *Means "up until the present time" and is standardly used with the present perfect when quantifying results or actions completed up to now.* |
